The three ideas you must actually understand

Not memorised. Understood, so you can say them two different ways

1. Loss says how bad. Error says which way.

Modrić hits one from 27 yards. Your model said 3.8%. It went in.

  • Loss = −ln(0.038) = 3.274. Big, because the model claimed that was unlikely and it happened. Always positive. It cannot tell you which way to move, only that you are hurting.
  • Error = p − y = 0.038 − 1 = −0.962. Negative, which by the way the subtraction is written means too low. So push up.

Positive error means the model was too high, come down. Negative means too low, go up. That is the entire direction mechanism.

2. How it knows which dial to turn

Same error for every weight. Different feature value, so a different share each.

That last row is the answer to "how does it know". A feature that is zero contributes nothing, so its weight does not move. The header weight only ever learns from headers, the counter-attack weight only from counters. Each dial quietly accumulates evidence from the shots that involve it.

3. Calibration and resolution, via the weather

Think of a forecaster.

  • Calibration (also called reliability, measured by ECE): across every day they said 30%, did it rain about 30% of the time? That is honesty. Yours is 0.0245, so your stated percentages are off by about two and a half points on average.
  • Resolution (also called sharpness): do they ever actually commit? A forecaster who says 9% every single day is perfectly honest and totally useless. Yours went from 0.0083 to 0.0125 as features were added.

Your instinct was right: resolution is putting your money where your mouth is. And your finding in one line: adding features made the model sharper, not more honest, because honesty was nearly free from the start.

Why honesty was free

The model has a bias term, and log loss is a proper scoring rule. Between them, gradient descent drags the average prediction onto the base rate almost regardless of what the other weights do. Telling a 40% chance from a 5% chance is the part you actually pay for.

"Given one shot, output the probability it's a goal. In training the input is a matrix of shots and a 0/1 label, and the output is a weight vector. At deployment the input is one shot's features, scaled with the stored training statistics, and the output is one number between 0 and 1. No threshold, no label. The probability is the product."

Slide 2 · Why it is worth asking

"An xG model can't know if a shot goes in. Two identical chances end differently and neither is a mistake. What it's for is calibration: of the chances it calls 0.30, about 30% should go in. But it's trained on log loss over individual binary outcomes. Those aren't the same objective, and this project is about where they come apart."

2:15

Slide 3 · H, L, A on one slide

Hypothesis space: every function σ(w·x + b). Criterion: cross entropy plus L2, not on the bias. Algorithm: full-batch gradient descent, (1/n)Xᵀ(σ(Xw) − y) + 2λw.

"I started from the perceptron. Wrong output type, it gives a hard label, and shot data isn't separable so it never converges. Keep the same hypothesis space, change the criterion and the algorithm, and you have logistic regression."

Questions, answered

Roughly in order of how likely they are

How many features does your final model have?

35, plus a bias. Two geometry, four situation flags, three body part, two shot type, eleven for what created the chance, nine for phase of play, four from the freeze frame. Answer it instantly. Li uses this exact question to catch people who did not build their own project.

Why logistic regression and not a neural net or gradient boosting?

Two reasons. 7,451 shots with 663 goals means the effective sample size is set by the positives, so a high-capacity model mostly fits noise. And the project is about the criterion, so I needed a model where behaviour is attributable to the loss rather than to capacity. The honest cost is that every interaction has to be hand-built, and that is most of the remaining gap to StatsBomb.

Why cross entropy rather than squared error?

It is a proper scoring rule, so in expectation it is minimised when the predicted probability equals the true one. And its gradient does not vanish when the model is confidently wrong. I tested that: same model on squared error gave 0.2657 against 0.2568, and calibration error 0.0373 against 0.0245.

Why does that vanishing actually happen?

The sigmoid saturates. Out at 3.8% it is almost flat, so pushing z barely moves p. Squared error's gradient carries that flatness through as a p(1−p) factor, so the update goes quiet exactly where the model is most wrong. Cross entropy's slope is −1/p, which blows up at the same rate, so the two cancel and what is left is plain error times input.

Why don't you penalise the bias?

The bias sets the base rate. Shrinking it drags every prediction towards 0.5, and goals happen 9% of the time.

Why split by match rather than by shot?

Shots in one match share a keeper, a pitch, a scoreline and a defensive shape, so they are not independent draws. Splitting by shot puts near-copies on both sides of the fence and the test estimate comes out flattering. That is leakage.

Your accuracy is barely above the baseline. Isn't that bad?

It is the point. Predicting "no goal" every time scores 90.3%. A metric where modelling nothing captures almost all of the score is not measuring the thing. That is why I judge on log loss, Brier and calibration.

Why 0.5 for the learning rate?

Empirical. I tried a few and watched the loss curves. There is a principled bound involving the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian and I did not compute it. What I can say is the loss decreased monotonically and my solution agrees with sklearn's LBFGS to 0.0074 on the largest weight. This is in my implementation log as a stated knowledge gap.

How do you know your gradient is right?

Central differences against the analytic gradient on random data, maximum relative error 4.55e-10. It runs in the notebook every time. A wrong gradient still trains, it just trains to the wrong place, so the check is not optional.

Did AI write this?

Yes, and section 4.2 of my log says exactly what it did. It wrote the download boilerplate, the plotting and the pandas grouping, and it suggested the Murphy decomposition and the point-in-triangle test. It also handed me a version that fitted the standardiser before the split, which is leakage, and I caught it. The xG framing and the calibration question are mine.
